## Learn words, not characters

A kanji learned alone is a shape attached to a vague English word and one or two
sounds. It fades in a week. The same kanji learned inside three words you can read
sticks, because each word reinforces the reading and pins the meaning to something
concrete.

This is why every page here puts **common words** before anything else and why the
example sentences use those specific words. Read the words first. Come back to the
reading list afterwards, and it will mostly already be familiar.

## Frequency order beats list order

Each level page is ordered by newspaper frequency rank, not by stroke count or
Unicode order. That ordering matters more than it looks: the top of an N2 list
appears constantly in real text, and the bottom appears a few times a year. If you
have limited time, working down from the top is strictly better than working
through alphabetically.

## On'yomi and kun'yomi, practically

The rule that actually predicts pronunciation most of the time:

- A kanji **on its own**, or followed by hiragana okurigana, usually takes its
  **kun'yomi**. 新しい → あたらしい.
- A kanji **glued to another kanji** usually takes its **on'yomi**.
  新聞 → しんぶん.

It is not a law — 手紙 is てがみ, two kanji with kun readings — but as a first guess
it is right far more often than not, and it turns a list of readings into a decision
you can make while reading.

## What changes at N2

N2 is where kanji stop being picturable and start being *systematic*. Most N2
characters have one dominant on'yomi and little useful kun'yomi, which sounds easier
and is, for reading aloud. The difficulty moves somewhere else: **telling similar
characters apart**. 税 / 説 / 脱 share a component and differ by one radical. So do
複 / 復 / 腹.

When two kanji are confusable, learn them together and deliberately — meeting the
second one cold, months later, is how the confusion sets in. The radical shown in
each page's header is the part that actually distinguishes them.

## A workable loop

1. Read the level page top to bottom once, just to see the shapes.
2. Take five kanji. For each, read the common words aloud, then the sentences.
3. Cover the readings and try to read the words again from the kanji alone.
4. The next day, start with those five before adding five more.

Twenty minutes daily beats three hours on Sunday. Kanji retention is a function of
how many separate days you have met a character, not how many total minutes.
