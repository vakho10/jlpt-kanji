## The two level lists

Which kanji belong to which JLPT level is taken from two public references:

- [kanji.jepang.org](https://kanji.jepang.org/en/jlpt/n2) — swap the level segment for `n1`, `n3`, `n4`, `n5`
- [kanjilibrary.com](https://kanjilibrary.com/kanji/n2/) — swap the level segment the same way

They hold different numbers of kanji:

| Level | kanji.jepang.org | kanjilibrary.com | Only on jepang |
|:------|-----------------:|-----------------:|---------------:|
| N5    |               79 |               79 |              — |
| N4    |              167 |              166 |              1 |
| N3    |              416 |              367 |             49 |
| N2    |              373 |              367 |              6 |
| N1    |          1,249\* |            1,232 |             17 |
| Total |            2,284 |            2,211 |             73 |

\* kanji.jepang.org's N1 page reports 1,250, but serves 1,249 distinct characters.

## They differ by omission, not by disagreement

This is worth stating plainly, because the counts above suggest a bigger problem
than there is. Comparing the two lists character by character:

- Every one of kanjilibrary.com's **2,211** kanji appears on kanji.jepang.org.
- Every one of them is placed at **exactly the same level** by both sites.
- kanji.jepang.org simply lists **73 more** characters that kanjilibrary.com
  does not carry at all.

So there is not a single kanji the two sources place at different levels. Where
they differ, one source is silent rather than contradicting the other. Those 73
kanji carry a note on their own page saying so; this site follows the longer
list, so nothing is dropped.

## Why any of this is reconstructed

Until 2009 the JLPT published an official kanji list for each level. The 2010
redesign dropped it deliberately: the exam now tests whether you can read real
Japanese, not whether you memorised a fixed set. The organisers publish no list
at all.

Every list you will find, including both of the ones above, is therefore a
**reconstruction** — built from past papers, textbook coverage and frequency data.
The N3 gap is the largest because N3 was added in the redesign and has the least
historical data behind it.

<div class="alert alert-success" markdown="1">
**What this means for studying**


Do not chase the last twenty kanji of a level. The difference lives entirely
in the low-frequency tail, and that tail is where the lists are least
trustworthy and the exam least likely to lean. Frequency order — the order the
level pages on this site use — is a better guide than list membership.
</div>

<div class="alert alert-warning" markdown="1">
**N1 is the remainder bucket**


Both reconstructions assign N5 through N2 first and sweep everything else into
N1, which is why N1 holds over 1,200 characters — more than the other four
levels combined. Genuinely common kanji end up there for no reason other than
that no lower list claimed them: 第 (frequency rank #160), 結 (#162) and
保 (#146) are all listed as N1 by both sources, despite being far more common
than plenty of N2 characters. An N1 tag here means "not on a lower list", not
"rare".
</div>

## Readings, meanings and vocabulary

- **KANJIDIC2** — readings, meanings, stroke counts, radicals, school grade and
  newspaper frequency ranks.
- **JMdict** — vocabulary and glosses for the common-words section.

Both are maintained by the [Electronic Dictionary Research and Development
Group](https://www.edrdg.org/) and used under a Creative Commons licence.

## Example sentences

Most sentences come from the **Tanaka corpus**, the Japanese–English sentence-pair
collection distributed by the EDRDG and maintained today as part of
[Tatoeba](https://tatoeba.org/). For each kanji the site picks the shortest
sentence that uses one of its listed words and contains no kanji from a *harder*
level than the one being studied — so an N5 example will not quietly lean on an
N1 character.

<div class="alert alert-secondary" markdown="1">
**What that does and does not guarantee**


The filter controls sentence length and kanji difficulty. It cannot judge
grammar, so an example may still use a construction from above its level, and
the readings and romaji under each sentence are generated by morphological
analysis rather than checked by hand. Around 280 kanji — almost all of them
rare N1 characters — have no sentence yet, and say so on their page.
</div>

## Stroke order

Stroke-order diagrams are rendered from [KanjiVG](https://github.com/KanjiVG/kanjivg),
which ships both the stroke paths and their numbering, so the diagram and the
numbers can never drift apart.
